# Chapter Three: The Love That Transforms

We have looked at what the wrong Jesus lacks — the sharp edges, the uncomfortable demands, the lordship that does not negotiate. But there is a danger in overcorrection. A Jesus reduced to demanding authority is as distorted as a Jesus reduced to therapeutic affirmation. The Jesus of the Gospels is not primarily a law-giver, not primarily an authority-figure, and not primarily a moral philosopher. He is primarily someone who loves, and who loves in ways that prove consistently more surprising than any of his followers seem to anticipate.

## The Quality of the Love

The love Jesus demonstrates in the Gospels is not what we might expect from a teacher of strict demands. It seeks out the people the culture has written off. It is tender with those who are broken and fierce with those who are proud. It makes people feel seen in unsettling ways — seen not as they present themselves, but as they actually are — and yet they do not always flee from that seeing. Something in them recognizes that to be known this way is to be freed.

Consider the woman at the well in John 4. This is a woman who has come at noon — the hottest hour, when no one else would be drawing water — presumably to avoid the social shame that attaches to her. And Jesus speaks with her, which is already strange (Jewish men did not typically converse with Samaritan women in public), and then he names the life she has been living, the five husbands, the man who is not her husband. He is not accusatory. He is simply accurate. And the effect of that accuracy is not shame but liberation. She becomes, in John's telling, one of the most effective evangelists in the Gospel — running into town to say *Come and see a man who told me everything I ever did.*

This is what the love of the actual Jesus does. It does not flinch from truth, but truth in its hands is not a weapon. It is a doorway.

## The Problem with Cheap Grace

There is a phrase that has been widely used since the German theologian Dietrich Bonhoeffer coined it in the middle of the last century: *cheap grace*. Bonhoeffer meant by it the kind of forgiveness that is offered without repentance, the kind of grace that costs nothing and therefore changes nothing. He was writing in Germany in the early years of the Third Reich, watching the church accommodate itself to atrocity in the name of a gospel that had been drained of its demands.

The cheap grace problem is, if anything, more acute now. We live in a therapeutic culture that has strong opinions about affirmation and very weak opinions about transformation. The notion that love might insist on change — that it might look, in fact, a great deal like what the New Testament calls repentance — strikes many contemporary ears as an intrusion of judgment where judgment does not belong.

But Jesus's love consistently refuses this dichotomy. *Go and sin no more* is not a contradiction of *Neither do I condemn you.* It is its completion. The woman caught in adultery is not simply released from judgment and left as she was. She is released from judgment into a new possibility: a different life, a transformed future. The love that refuses to condemn her is the same love that refuses to let her remain unchanged.

## What Transformation Looks Like

This raises the hardest question of all. If the love of Jesus transforms, what does that transformation actually look like? What are we being transformed into?

The New Testament is consistent on this point, even when its images vary. We are being conformed to the image of the Son. We are being formed into the pattern of cruciform love — the love that gives itself, that refuses retaliation, that persists in mercy even when mercy is costly. We are being made, in the fullest theological sense of the phrase, into people who love as Jesus loves.

This is not self-improvement. Self-improvement targets our worst habits and replaces them with better ones. What Jesus is after is something more radical: a reorientation of the self from its center, a redirection of the will from self-preservation toward self-offering. This is why Paul can use the language of death and resurrection to describe Christian formation. The self does not get better; it gets crucified and raised.

## A Different Story Than We Thought

What this book has tried to do, in these three chapters, is sketch the outlines of a different story than the one many of us inherited. Not a story of a Jesus who merely affirms us where we are. Not a story of a Jesus whose only interest is our compliance with a set of moral rules. But a story of a Jesus whose love is radical enough to tell us the truth about ourselves, whose authority is real enough to make actual demands, and whose transforming work is deep enough to reach places that no program of self-improvement has ever touched.

This Jesus is more difficult than the one we thought we knew. He is also, as the disciples seem to have discovered with something like relief, more worth following.

The wrong Jesus is too small. The real one is exactly the size of our need.
