If we know all the answers before we move, we are not walking in faith. We are walking in knowledge. Knowledge is a good thing, and answers are worth loving, but faith lives in a different place. Faith is what remains when God asks for a step and does not first explain how the pieces fit together.

Abraham climbed Mount Moriah carrying two words from God that appeared to contradict each other. God had told him plainly that the covenant would run through Isaac. Isaac would inherit the promise, and Isaac's children would inherit it after him. Then God told him to take that same son to a mountain and offer him there (Genesis 22:2). Both words came from the same God. Abraham could not see a way for both to be true, and he climbed anyway.

It helps to notice what Abraham said before the final ascent. He told his servants to stay behind with the donkey while he and the boy went ahead to worship, and he told them that both of them would return (Genesis 22:5). Abraham said that with the command to sacrifice already ringing in his ears. He was not bluffing his servants and he was not reciting a religious phrase. He was stating what he trusted: we will worship, and we will come back. The promise had not been cancelled by the command, and so he held both.

We should be careful here not to read the end of the story back into the middle of it. We know there is a ram caught in a thicket, because we get to read the whole account. Watching a film to the end and then second guessing the characters twenty minutes in is easy work. Abraham had no advance copy. He did not know about the ram. What he knew for certain was that God had promised descendants through Isaac, and that God does not lie.

This is where a particular verse deserves care. Isaac asks where the lamb for the burnt offering is, and Abraham answers that God will provide the lamb (Genesis 22:8). English translations add the word will, because English demands a conjugated verb to complete the sentence. The Hebrew has no such verb in that spot. Our reading is that a more natural rendering may be that God is providing the lamb. That is a reading of the grammar, not a settled fact, and it matters because the common English version can make Abraham sound like he had inside information about the ram. A human being does not have the future. What Abraham had was a settled conviction about the One who does.

Some interpreters take this further and argue that Abraham was already trusting in resurrection, that even if the knife fell, a God powerful enough to give a son to a hundred year old man could raise him again. That is a beautiful picture, and it preaches well now that we have the resurrection of Jesus in hand. But the text does not tell us Abraham had worked out the mechanics, and we should not claim more than it says. What we can say with confidence is this: Abraham knew they were going up, he knew they would worship, and he knew they were coming down. He did not know how God would reconcile the promise with the instruction.

That is the pattern for our own obedience. We tend to demand the how before we grant the trust. We want the plan laid out, the finances mapped, the outcome secured, and the tension between what God said and what we see resolved on paper. But faith is not the resolution of tension. It is the willingness to walk into it holding on to the character of the One who spoke.

Sometimes we have to trust the Who because we cannot understand the how. There are seasons in every life when God has said two things that do not seem to fit together. He has promised to provide, and the account is empty. He has promised to be near, and the room is silent. He has called us to something, and every door appears shut. The question in those seasons is not whether we can construct an explanation. The question is whether the God who made the promise is trustworthy enough to keep it through a path we cannot see.

Abraham had a track record with God that made the answer easy to give and costly to live. He had watched God open a barren womb. He had watched God keep His word after decades of delay. That history did not remove the ache of Moriah, but it gave the ache somewhere to rest. Trust is rarely built in a single moment on a single mountain. It is built in the long accumulation of small, kept promises that teach us who God is before we are asked to bet everything on it.

Whatever tension we are carrying today, we can take the next step with the same steadiness. We do not need to explain how the promise and the instruction will meet. We need to know Who made both, and to keep walking.
