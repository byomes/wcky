There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from pretending we have arrived somewhere we have not actually arrived. We feel it in conversations where we nod along as though our faith is fully settled, in prayers we say publicly that sound more resolved than our hearts actually are, in the quiet pressure to present a version of ourselves that has already figured out how to consistently live for Christ.

Paul refuses to play that game. In the middle of one of the most passionate passages he ever wrote about knowing Christ, he stops and says plainly that he has not already obtained this, that he has not already arrived at his goal. He goes further and says that he does not even consider himself to have taken hold of it yet. This admission comes from a man who had given up everything for Christ, who had suffered imprisonment for the sake of the gospel, and who would eventually be martyred for his faith. If Paul had not arrived, none of us should expect to have arrived either.

There is real freedom in this kind of honesty. Paul is not describing failure. He is describing the gap between where his heart wants to be and where his life currently is, and he is naming that gap without shame. He wants to know Christ so much that he would suffer for Him, die for Him, and be found faithful to the end. He also knows that his daily life does not fully reflect that desire yet. Both things are true at the same time, and Paul does not try to resolve the tension by pretending the gap does not exist.

We often assume that admitting this gap makes us hypocrites. It does not. Hypocrisy is claiming to be something we are not while making no effort to actually become it. Paul is doing the opposite. He is honestly naming where he falls short while actively pressing forward toward the goal. The honesty is not a substitute for pursuit. It is what makes real pursuit possible, because we cannot press toward a goal we refuse to admit we have not reached.

This matters because many of us live with a quiet fear that our inconsistency disqualifies us. We have days where we feel close to Christ, energized to serve, eager to open Scripture, and we have other days where none of that feels true, where obedience feels like duty rather than desire, where we would rather rest than pray. We interpret those inconsistent days as evidence that our faith is not real. Paul would tell us something different. He would tell us that wanting Christ more than our current life evidences is not hypocrisy. It is simply honesty about where we are while we continue moving forward.

Forgetting what is behind and straining toward what is ahead does not mean forgetting our failures in a way that pretends they never happened. It means refusing to let past failure or past success define our present pursuit. We are not measured by how far behind we have fallen on a given day. We are measured by whether we are still straining forward, still pressing on, still refusing to settle into whatever level of devotion currently feels comfortable.

This posture protects us from two opposite dangers. The first is despair, the belief that because we have not arrived, we might as well stop trying, since perfection feels impossibly far away. The second is a false sense of arrival, the belief that because we have grown in some areas, we no longer need to keep pressing forward in others. Paul avoids both. He holds real conviction about how far he still has to go, and he holds real determination to keep going anyway.

We should learn to speak about our own faith with this same combination of honesty and determination. We can say plainly that we do not yet love Christ the way we want to, that our obedience is inconsistent, that some days our hearts feel far from Him even when our schedules say we showed up. That admission does not disqualify us from pursuing Him further. It is often the very thing that keeps the pursuit honest, because we stop performing a finished product and start living as people still in process, still straining, still pressing on toward a goal we have not yet reached but refuse to stop chasing.

The gap Paul names is not a problem to hide. It is simply where growth happens, and admitting it honestly is the first step toward closing it, day after day, for as long as Christ gives us breath to keep pressing forward.
