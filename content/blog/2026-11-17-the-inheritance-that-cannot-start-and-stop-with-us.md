The word legacy carries its own definition. A legacy is something handed down, and that means it cannot begin and end in one life. If it stops with us, it was an accomplishment. If it started with us and we never passed it on, it was only a beginning. A legacy needs at least two generations to earn the name, and the faith God gives was never designed to stay in the person who received it.

We can see this in the way God first spoke to Abraham about His covenant. He promised to establish an everlasting covenant between Himself and Abraham, and between Himself and Abraham's descendants after him, for generations to come, to be their God (Genesis 17:7). Notice how far that sentence reaches. God was not making a private arrangement with one man. He was building a river, and Abraham was only the spring where it surfaced. Whatever God was about to do in that one life was directed and purposed to flow through it into lives Abraham would never meet.

Abraham did not respond with a clean, immediate yes. He fell face down and laughed, asking himself whether a man a hundred years old could father a son, and whether Sarah could bear a child at ninety (Genesis 17:17). Then he offered God an alternative that sounded reasonable: if only Ishmael might live under God's blessing (Genesis 17:18). We should not be quick to mock him for it. Ishmael was his firstborn son, and any parent knows what it is to love a child with that kind of ferocity. Abraham was not scheming. He was asking God to bless the child already standing in front of him.

But Ishmael was the result of a detour. God had promised a son through Sarah, and when the waiting became unbearable, Abraham and Sarah tried to help God along by way of Hagar. That was never God's plan. It was a human shortcut, taken by people who did not have the patience to let God keep His own word in His own time. So when Abraham asks God to attach the covenant to the son he already has, God answers plainly. Sarah will bear a son, his name will be Isaac, and the everlasting covenant will be established with him and with his descendants after him (Genesis 17:19).

Two things become clear from that exchange. First, the covenant was always meant to be inherited, and it was always meant to travel a specific road: from Abraham to Isaac to Jacob. God chose the channel. Abraham did not get to redirect it, however sincere his motives. This is why the popular claim that Judaism, Christianity, and Islam are essentially one faith because they share an ancestor collapses under the text itself. Sharing a father is not the same as sharing a promise, and God said exactly where His promise would run.

Second, God does not hand a legacy to just anyone who happens to be nearby and beloved. Faith is not passed along by affection or by good intentions. It is passed along by the pattern God sets, and our job is to cooperate with that pattern rather than improve on it. Abraham loved Ishmael, and God still said no. Some of the hardest lessons in following Him come when God declines to bless the thing we love because He has already named something better.

There is a sobering thought in all of this for anyone who sits in a pew, a classroom, or a living room today. Every one of us is an inheritor. Someone taught us to pray before we understood what prayer was. Someone told us the story of a God who keeps His promises before we had reason to test it. Someone stood in a doorway of faith and held it open long enough for us to walk through. Whatever we hold of the gospel, we did not invent it. We received it from hands we may never have thanked, and those hands received it from hands before them.

That reframes what we owe. Faith is a gift, and gifts carry a shape. A gift received and then quietly buried stops being a legacy and becomes a private possession. God's design from the first chapters of the covenant is that what He does in one life is aimed past it, at children, at grandchildren, at people who are not born yet and who will need the same God who kept His word to Abraham.

The good news is that the God who built the river is the One who keeps it flowing. Abraham laughed, offered an alternative, and got a plain correction, and God did not abandon the plan because His servant struggled to believe it. He simply kept it. He is at work in our weak, laughing, second guessing lives as well, and He is faithful to carry His promise forward through the ordinary people willing to hand it on.
