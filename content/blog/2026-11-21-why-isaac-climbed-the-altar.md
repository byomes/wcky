One of the most common objections to the Christian faith goes like this: the God of the Bible tells a father to kill his son, so either God approves of child sacrifice or the Bible cannot be trusted. It is an old charge, and it sounds strong when it is shouted in a sentence. It weakens considerably when the story is read in full, because the account itself supplies the answer.

Begin with what God had already said. Before Moriah, God told Abraham in unmistakable terms that the covenant would run through Isaac. When Abraham asked God to bless Ishmael instead, God refused and named Isaac as the son through whom the promise would continue (Genesis 17:19). Isaac was not a random child selected for a grim task. He was the son of promise, the very person through whom God had pledged to bless the nations. The command to offer him did not arrive in a vacuum. It arrived on top of a promise, and the promise did not disappear when the command was given. This is precisely why the account is a test of trust and not an instruction manual for parents.

Next, consider the picture the text paints of Isaac himself. Many of us grew up with a Sunday school image of a small child snatched up by a frightening father. The passage does not support that image. Abraham took the wood for the burnt offering and laid it on Isaac, and the two of them walked on together (Genesis 22:6). A boy small enough to be snatched up would not be hauling the wood for a burnt offering up a mountain. The text does not state Isaac's age, and we should say so plainly. But reading it carefully, the reasonable conclusion is that Isaac was a young man, strong enough to carry the load his father put on him. Abraham was a hundred years old when Isaac was born (Genesis 21:5), which means that whatever Isaac's age at Moriah, his father was well past a hundred. That is our reasoning from the text, not a stated number, and it points in one direction.

Follow that reasoning to its conclusion. When Abraham built the altar, arranged the wood, and bound his son, an elderly man was doing this to a young, able one (Genesis 22:9). A man of that age does not chase down, overpower, and tie up someone in his prime. If Isaac had resisted, the scene could not have unfolded as it did. What the narrative describes is a son who allowed himself to be bound.

Then listen to Isaac's question on the way up. He noticed that the fire and the wood were present, and he asked where the lamb was (Genesis 22:7). That question tells us something significant. Isaac knew how a burnt offering worked, which means Abraham had already taught him how to worship. He recognized that a piece of the sacrifice was missing, and he asked his father about it with confidence that his father would answer honestly. This is not a terrified child. This is a son who trusted his father because his father had shown him what it looked like to trust God.

Put it together and the objection changes shape. The story is not about a tyrant demanding blood. It is about a father and son walking together in obedience to a God who had already promised to bless the world through that son. Then, at the moment the knife is raised, God stops it. The account does not end in a sacrifice. It ends in a provision. A God who delighted in child sacrifice would not have intervened. The intervention is the point of the story, and it is the point the objection skips.

The account also reaches forward. A beloved son carries wood up a hill, submits willingly, and is laid on an altar, while a substitute is provided in his place. We would be right to see in it a shadow of Jesus, the Son of God who willingly gave Himself for our sins. The connection is real. But the account does not need the New Testament to defend itself against the charge. Read on its own terms, it shows a covenant God who keeps His promise, a father who trusts Him, and a son who trusts his father.

Anyone who wants to dismiss the Bible with a single verse must reckon with all the surrounding verses. If the passage is true, then the promise before it is true, the provision after it is true, and the trust running through the middle is the whole point. Isaac climbed the altar because he had learned, from a father who had learned it from God, that the One who asks is also the One who provides.
