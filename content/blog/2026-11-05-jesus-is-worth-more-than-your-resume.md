We spend our lives building resumes. Some of them are professional: degrees earned, promotions won, titles collected. But we build spiritual resumes too, and this is far more dangerous, because we mistake spiritual achievement for spiritual standing.

Paul had a resume that could stop a room. He was circumcised on the eighth day, born into the covenant rather than converted into it. He came from the tribe of Benjamin, one of only two tribes that remained loyal when the kingdom split. He was a Hebrew of Hebrews, a Pharisee trained in the strictest interpretation of the law, a man so zealous for God that he persecuted the church in the name of purity. By every external measure available to a first century Jew, he was faultless.

And he called all of it garbage.

Not garbage because it was fake. Not garbage because he had lied about his credentials. Garbage because none of it, however real, however impressive, could produce the one thing his soul actually needed: righteousness before God. Everything Paul had gained through birth, through discipline, through zeal, he now counted as loss for the sake of knowing Christ.

We should sit with how offensive this is to our instincts. We are trained from childhood to build resumes. We are taught that effort earns outcome, that discipline produces results, that the harder we work the more we deserve. This is true in almost every arena of life. It is not true in the arena of righteousness. No accumulation of religious performance, however sincere, can manufacture what only Christ can give.

This does not mean effort is meaningless or that spiritual disciplines do not matter. Paul did not stop reading Scripture or stop praying or stop serving the church after this realization. What changed was the foundation underneath those disciplines. He no longer practiced them to build a case for his own righteousness. He practiced them because he already possessed a righteousness that came from God on the basis of faith, and these were the natural overflow of knowing the One who had given it to him.

If we were honest, we could write our own version of Paul's list. Baptized before we could remember, raised in a Christian home, decades of church attendance without missing a Sunday, a working knowledge of the Bible sharpened over years of sermons and small groups. None of these are the problem. The problem comes when we begin to whisper to ourselves that this list is what makes us right with God, that our history in the church has purchased something in the eyes of Christ.

We are prone to build resumes in quieter ways than Paul's opponents did. We do not require circumcision, but we quietly require our own entry fees into acceptable Christianity. We measure ourselves by how long our quiet time was this morning, how many books of the Bible we can say we have read this year, how doctrinally sharp our answers are in a small group discussion. None of these things are wrong to pursue. They become dangerous the moment we use them to calculate our own worth or someone else's. The moment a spiritual discipline becomes a scoreboard, it has stopped pointing us to Christ and started pointing us to ourselves.

The remarkable thing about Paul's confession is not that he had accomplishments. It is that he was willing to set the accomplishments down. He uses the language of loss deliberately. Everything he had once counted as gain, he now counts as loss. The word carries the weight of a financial transaction gone wrong, an investment that returned nothing. Paul is saying that his entire resume, when placed next to Christ, was worthless. Not neutral. Worthless.

This is the posture every follower of Christ is invited into. Not the abandonment of effort, but the abandonment of self reliance. We are free to stop calculating our spiritual net worth by comparing our discipline to someone else's, our knowledge to someone else's, our track record to someone else's. There is no version of that comparison that ends in peace, because there is always someone who prays longer, reads more, serves harder, or knows more. The scale itself is broken. Paul did not win the comparison game and then walk away from it. He walked away from the game entirely, because he found something that made the game irrelevant.

The surpassing worth of knowing Christ is not a phrase meant to diminish the value of our lives, our work, or our growth. It is meant to reorder what we are actually resting on. Our résumé, however impressive, was never going to be enough. It was never designed to be enough. It was designed to be surrendered, so that something better could take its place. Christ Himself, and the righteousness that comes only through faith in Him, is the one foundation that does not crack under the weight of our failures or inflate under the weight of our achievements. Everything else, however good it looks on paper, is loss by comparison.
