Most of us instinctively read suffering as evidence that something has gone wrong. We assume that if we are truly walking with Christ, our lives should feel increasingly stable, increasingly comfortable, increasingly free of hardship. So when suffering comes, we often interpret it as a setback, a detour from the life we are supposed to be living.

Paul offers a radically different framework. Writing from a Roman prison, awaiting a legal outcome that could end in his execution, he does not describe his suffering as a setback. He describes it as part of an investment he has already made, one he is holding onto while he waits for the return to come due.

The word he uses for pressing on carries this financial weight. It is not primarily an athletic term, though it can be read that way. It describes the posture of someone who has made an investment and is now waiting, patiently and deliberately, for the reward that investment will eventually produce. An investment requires two things that suffering often forces on us whether we want them or not: time and endurance. We cannot make an investment and receive the return instantly. We have to hold the position through fluctuation, through uncertainty, sometimes through real loss, trusting that the value will eventually be realized.

Paul is saying that his suffering is not separate from his investment in Christ. It is part of the investment itself. He wants to know Christ, yes, but he specifically wants to know the power of His resurrection, and he understands that participating in Christ's sufferings is the path that leads there. He is not suffering instead of experiencing resurrection life. He is suffering on the way to it.

This reframes how we should think about our own hardship. When difficulty enters our lives, whether it is physical suffering, relational loss, financial strain, or simple daily weariness in following Christ, our instinct is to ask what went wrong. Paul would ask a different question. He would ask what this suffering is producing, what it is investing us toward, what return it might be building even while it feels like nothing but loss in the moment.

This is not a call to manufacture suffering or to treat hardship as something to be pursued for its own sake. Paul is not glorifying pain. He is reframing its purpose. Suffering endured for the sake of Christ is never wasted. It is part of the process by which we come to know Him more fully, and it is part of what we are holding onto while we wait for the resurrection He has promised.

Most of us in comfortable circumstances are not signing up for suffering. We prefer a version of Christ who comforts us, blesses us, and holds us when we are sad, and there is real truth in that picture of Him. But Paul reminds us that the same Christ who comforts us also calls us to participate in something costly, because the costly parts of following Him are where the deepest knowledge of Him is found. We do not get to the power of His resurrection by skipping past the fellowship of His sufferings. The two come as a pair.

This is also why patience matters so much in the Christian life. An investor who panics and sells the moment the market dips never sees the return they were waiting for. Paul is describing exactly that kind of patient holding, except the currency is faith and the market is the life we live for Christ under real pressure. He has not yet obtained the resurrection he is investing toward. He says so plainly. But he presses on anyway, holding the position, because he trusts the character of the One who called him to it.

We should ask ourselves whether we are treating our current hardships as senseless interruptions or as part of an investment we have made in knowing Christ. The answer changes how we endure. An interruption tempts us to escape as quickly as possible. An investment teaches us to hold on, even when holding on costs something, because we believe the return is real and it is coming.

Paul wrote these words from a cell, uncertain whether he would live or die, and still chose the language of investment rather than the language of defeat. That choice is instructive. Our suffering, when surrendered to Christ, is never simply loss. It is part of what we are holding onto until the day He calls the investment due.
