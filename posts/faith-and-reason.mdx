One of the most persistent myths in Western intellectual culture is the idea that faith and reason are fundamentally at odds — that to have religious faith is to disengage the critical faculties, to believe without (or against) evidence. This picture deserves to be challenged.

## A Caricature, Not a Description

The philosopher Alvin Plantinga has spent a career demonstrating that belief in God can be *rational* — that it can be properly basic, warranted, and coherent. His work in epistemology alone should give pause to anyone who assumes that religious belief is necessarily irrational.

But the challenge to the faith-versus-reason narrative goes deeper than any single philosopher's argument. It goes all the way back to the history of Western thought itself.

## The Historical Case

Consider that the great universities of medieval Europe were founded by the Church. Consider that some of the most rigorous medieval thinkers — Anselm, Aquinas, Scotus, Ockham — were Christian monks and friars who believed that *faith seeks understanding* (*fides quaerens intellectum*, in Anselm's famous phrase).

For these thinkers, faith and reason were not rivals. Faith provided the starting point — a relationship with the God who is Logos, who is reason itself. And reason was the tool by which that faith explored, clarified, and defended its own content.

## The Modern Confusion

The idea that faith and reason are enemies is largely a product of the 19th century and a particular reading of the so-called "conflict thesis" between science and religion. But historians of science have largely dismantled this thesis. The relationship between science and Christian theology has been far more complex, collaborative, and mutually generative than the conflict narrative suggests.

## Toward Integration

The Christian vision, properly understood, is not a retreat from reason but an invitation to use it fully. Faith, in the biblical sense, is not *believing without evidence*. It is *trusting on the basis of what one has come to know* — entrusting oneself to the God who has shown himself in history, in Scripture, and most fully in Jesus of Nazareth.

This is not a blind leap. It is a step taken with open eyes.
